Our story

A mission born from understanding.

Beyond the Mirror exists because body image struggles deserve compassion, credible information, and serious attention.

Why this became personal.

I created Beyond the Mirror because this mission is deeply personal to me. Someone who meant, and still means, a great deal to me experienced severe body dysmorphia and an eating disorder. Watching someone I cared about struggle with the way they saw themselves showed me how deeply these conditions can affect confidence, relationships, health, and everyday life.

I was born and raised in the Middle East, where mental health and body image struggles were not always openly discussed or fully understood. Growing up, I did not recognize how serious these conditions could be. Seeing their effects firsthand completely changed my perspective.

Beyond the Mirror was created from that experience. Its purpose is to challenge the idea that appearance related struggles are simply insecurity, reduce the stigma surrounding them, and make reliable information and trusted support easier to find.

Bashar ShalabiFounder, Beyond the Mirror Project

Understand the struggle

It is more than insecurity.

Body dysmorphic disorder can involve intense preoccupation with perceived appearance flaws, repeated behaviors, significant distress, and disruption to daily life.

What is body dysmorphic disorder?

Body dysmorphic disorder, often called BDD, is a serious mental health condition. A person can become deeply preoccupied with perceived flaws in their appearance that seem minor or may not be visible to other people.

BDD is not the same as occasionally feeling insecure. The concerns can cause intense distress and interfere with school, work, relationships, social life, and other valued activities.

Only a qualified health professional can diagnose BDD. Learning the signs can help someone recognize when it may be time to seek professional support.

Common experiences

What appearance distress can look like.

People experience BDD differently. These examples are educational and do not form a diagnosis.

Mirror checking or avoidanceRepeatedly checking a feature, or avoiding mirrors because seeing a reflection feels overwhelming.
ComparisonFrequently comparing appearance with other people in person or online.
CamouflagingUsing clothing, makeup, posture, grooming, or other methods to hide a perceived flaw.
Reassurance seekingRepeatedly asking others for confirmation about appearance without receiving lasting relief.
Repeated proceduresPursuing cosmetic or dermatological changes while the underlying distress continues.
Life disruptionAvoiding photographs, social situations, school, work, dating, or daily activities.

A useful distinction

Concern versus serious disruption.

The key difference is not whether someone dislikes a feature. It is the intensity, repetition, distress, and effect on daily functioning.

AreaOrdinary appearance concernPossible serious distress
AttentionConcern appears occasionally and can pass.Thoughts may feel consuming or difficult to control.
BehaviorsGrooming and checking remain flexible.Checking, avoiding, comparing, or concealing may feel repetitive or necessary.
Daily lifeActivities and relationships usually continue.School, work, social life, or relationships may be disrupted.
SupportSelf care and perspective may help.A qualified mental health professional may be needed.

Supporting someone else

Your response can make the moment feel less lonely.

Listen without arguing about whether the perceived flaw is real. The distress is real, even when you see the person's appearance differently.

Try saying, “I can hear how painful this is for you” or “You do not have to handle this alone.” Encourage qualified support without turning the conversation into pressure or an ultimatum.

Avoid repeated appearance reassurance, criticism, jokes, or comparisons. Instead, focus on the person's feelings, wellbeing, and the activities or relationships the struggle is affecting.

If you believe someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support.

Accessibility

Support should be easier to reach.

Beyond the Mirror is being built for clear navigation, readable content, keyboard access, and motion preferences.

Last updated: July 31, 2026

Our commitment

We want the site to be usable by as many people as possible, including visitors who use screen readers, keyboard navigation, magnification, or reduced motion settings.

Current features

  • Visible headings and consistent page structure
  • Keyboard accessible links, buttons, filters, and menus
  • Alternative text for meaningful images
  • High contrast black, ivory, and gold presentation
  • Responsive layouts for phones, tablets, and larger screens
  • Reduced animation for visitors who enable reduced motion

Ongoing work

Accessibility is not a one time task. We will continue testing content, contrast, focus states, forms, mobile navigation, and external resources as the project grows.

Report a barrier

If something is difficult to read, navigate, understand, or operate, please use the contact page and choose “Website correction.”
